# Survival Skills Field Manual

> Era guides (e.g. the [[xia-dynasty|Xia Dynasty]]) tell you **what** you must be able to do;
> this guide tells you **how, exactly**. You can bring nothing — every skill below relies only
> on materials you will find after arrival, plus the motor memory in your hands.

**The headline first**: "knowing the name" is not "being able to do it." A memorized list of
plant names is worthless if you cannot recognize the leaf; perfect understanding of friction-fire
physics produces no ember without calluses. This manual breaks each skill down to operable
granularity and ends with **pre-departure training standards**. Text gets you started;
only practice gets you alive.

## 1. Medicinal and Edible Plant Identification

### The three-factor rule

Always identify by the mutual confirmation of **form + habitat + season**:

1. **Form**: leaf arrangement, leaf shape, veins, sap at the break, flower/fruit — primary evidence.
2. **Habitat**: every plant has its home. Plantain grows on trampled paths; mugwort on sunny
   wastelands and field ridges. A "plantain" growing in the wrong place is something else.
3. **Season**: without flowers or fruit, the combination of leaf form, plant height, and
   shoot/stem maturity is key.

### Medicinal plants (memorize first)

| Plant | Identification | Habitat | Use (improvised materials only) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mugwort (艾草) | 50–100cm tall; pinnately lobed leaves, **dense grey-white hairs on the underside**; strong distinctive scent when crushed | Roadsides, field ridges, sunny slopes, in patches | Crushed leaves as a hemostatic poultice; smoke to repel insects; washed-broth for itchy skin |
| Plantain (车前草) | **Flat rosette** hugging the ground; ovate leaves with 5–7 parallel curved veins; slender upright spike like a rat's tail | Paths, fields, anywhere trampled | Whole plant brewed, sipped warm for mild diarrhea; young leaves edible |
| Dandelion (蒲公英) | Basal rosette of backward-lobed leaves; **white milky sap at the break**; yellow flower, then white puffball | Fields, roadsides, grassland | Whole plant edible after blanching (bitter); traditional cooling use |
| Willow bark | Tree or shrub with narrow leaves; grey-brown furrowed bark | Riverbanks (also marks water for you) | Bark brewed contains **salicin** (aspirin precursor) — the "ancient aspirin" for fever and pain. Small concentrated cup; overdose irritates the stomach |

**Memory hooks**: mugwort = white-haired leaf underside + scent; plantain = flat on the ground
+ parallel veins; dandelion = white sap + puffball. Related Artemisia species share mugwort's
uses, so misidentification within the genus is low-risk — that is why it tops the list.

### Edible wild greens (Central Plains / northern destinations)

| Plant | Identification | Preparation and warnings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shepherd's purse (荠菜) | Early-spring flat rosette, lobed leaves, small white flowers, **heart-shaped seed pods** | Whole plant edible; best in early spring |
| Goosefoot / fat hen (藜) | Powder-white bloom on young leaf undersides; young stems often red-striped | Blanch before eating; **large amounts plus sun exposure cause photosensitive poisoning** — small cooked portions are fine |
| Purslane (马齿苋) | Prostrate, **fleshy spatulate leaves**, reddish stems, sour taste | Edible raw or cooked |
| Bracken fiddleheads (蕨菜) | Young leaves curled like a question mark | **Must be thoroughly cooked** (contains carcinogenic ptaquiloside); occasional use only, never a staple |

### The poison blacklist (recognizing these saves more lives than recognizing medicine)

- **Aconite (乌头)**: carrot-like tuber, blue-purple hooded flowers, **entire plant deadly**
  (aconitine) — even sap on mucous membranes is dangerous.
- **Castor bean (蓖麻)**: attractively patterned seeds; ricin makes **a few seeds lethal**.
- **Gelsemium / "heartbreak grass" (钩吻)**: twining vine, yellow trumpet flowers, entire plant deadly.
- **Hemlock (毒芹)**: wet ground, hollow purple-spotted stalk, mouse-urine/rotten odor —
  **trace amounts are lethal**.

### The iron rule

**No folk rhyme reliably separates edible from poisonous** ("white sap is toxic,"
"bright colors are toxic" — both have countless counterexamples). There is one rule:
**eat only species you can confirm 100%.** As a last-resort fallback, the classic
escalating trial: rub sap on skin, wait 15 minutes → lip contact, wait → tongue touch, spit →
chew and spit → swallow a trace, wait 8 hours → eat a small amount, wait 24 hours;
advance only if each step is uneventful. **Note: this trial does not protect against
trace-lethal species like hemlock and aconite — confirmation always comes first.**

## 2. Fire-Making (Bow Drill), Step by Step

### Materials (all improvised)

- **Hearth board**: dry, soft, light wood (willow, poplar, catalpa); ~30cm long, palm-wide,
  5cm thick. Carve a thumbnail-sized depression with a **V-notch** at its edge (lets the
  char powder drop out).
- **Spindle**: straight, dry twig, 20–25cm, thumb-thick, lower end rounded — **ideally the
  same species as the board**.
- **Bow**: 60cm flexible branch + hemp cord/reed bark/leather/sinew.
- **Bearing block**: hard stone or hardwood with a lubricated (animal fat) socket — so the
  friction happens at the bottom, not under your hand.
- **Tinder nest**: bone-dry cattail fluff, crushed dry grass, punk-wood powder, inner
  birch-bark fiber.
- **Char catcher**: bark chip or thin wood under the V-notch.

### Procedure

1. Kneel on one knee and **anchor the board firmly under your foot** — instability is the
   root of all failure.
2. Wrap the bow cord around the spindle **1.5–2 turns**; seat the spindle's tip in the
   bearing block, pressing down vertically with the left hand.
3. Start with slow, short strokes to find stability, then **long, even strokes** with
   gradually increasing downward pressure.
4. Once smoking, **keep going 20–30 more seconds** harder; stop and lift out the char.
5. Tip the char into the tinder nest, fold and squeeze gently, blow **slow and even** —
   move the flame into the kindling stack at first light.
6. Escalate fuel: tinder → match-thick twigs → pencil-thick → finger-thick. Skipping a
   grade extinguishes everything.

### Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Smoke but no char | Too little downward pressure, or tinder not dry enough |
| Spindle slips | Bow cord loose — re-wrap |
| Bearing block smoking | Not lubricated — heat is happening in the wrong place; add fat |
| Blisters | Posture error (press vertically) — build calluses before departure |

**The humidity trap**: after rain, everything fails. Dead-branch tips still on the tree are
drier than fallen wood; after arrival, dry a backup fire-kit by the hearth. **Reality check**:
in most eras fire is *preserved*, not made daily (banking embers — see
[Ancient Winter Survival](/en/guides/winter-survival/)). Your value is being able to
**reignite a dead fire from nothing** — which reads as a miracle.

## 3. Pottery, Step by Step

1. **Find clay**: river-bend or gully-wall deposits. Field test: roll a finger-thick coil,
   bend it around your finger — **no cracking** means acceptable plasticity. Too sandy?
   Slake in water, let settle, skim the fine top layer and stiffen it back to workability.
2. **Temper**: add 10–20% fine sand, crushed shell, or grog (crushed pottery) — untempered
   clay explodes in the fire.
3. **Form (coiling)**: roll coils, stack and pinch-weld the seams, scrape the inner wall
   dense. No wheel needed.
4. **Dry slowly**: cover with a damp cloth for days. **No direct sun, no wind** — fast
   drying always cracks.
5. **Fire (open bonfire)**: pre-warm the pot at the fire's edge, then build a fuel bonfire
   around it and burn 2–3 hours (bonfires reach 600–900°C). Sound test when cool:
   **ringing** = fired through; **dull thud** = under-fired.
6. **Failure analysis**: spalling = not fully dry / heated too fast / under-tempered.

**Reality check**: village pottery is a mature craft — **apprenticing to a potter beats
self-teaching by months**. Your edge is knowledge: kiln improvements, carbon-trapping
blackware — someone who understands principles learns in a week what takes others a year.

## 4. Cordage, Knots, and Weaving

### Making cordage (two-strand reverse twist)

Fiber sources: hemp bast, reed bark, kudzu vine bark, elm inner bark, animal sinew.
Soak and pound to separate the fibers, then anchor one end and **twist both strands in the
same direction** — they wrap around each other naturally. Three strands are stronger.
The finished cord should hold your body weight.

### Four core knots (must become muscle memory)

1. **Bowline** (fixed loop, stays easy to untie after loading) — mnemonic "the rabbit comes
   out of the hole, around the tree, back down the hole": up through the loop → around the
   standing line → back down the loop → tighten. Uses: waist rescue loop, any fixed-loop need.
2. **Clove hitch** (attaching rope to a bar/stake) — full wrap crossing over itself, second
   wrap threading **over the first wrap**, tighten. Balanced pull both sides; the workhorse
   for racks and beams.
3. **Taut-line hitch** (adjustable tension) — two turns around the line → a third turn on
   top of the first two → half hitch to lock. Slides for tensioning: guys, racks, shelter frames.
4. **Square (reef) knot** (joining equal cords) — "left over right, then right over left";
   ends emerge on the same side. **Lethal warning**: tying the same direction twice gives a
   granny knot, which slips under load; for unequal cords use the sheet bend
   (thin rope loops around thick, tuck back under itself).

### Weaving

- **Baskets**: cross-lay the warp spokes → weave the weft → bend spokes up and continue
  up the walls → tuck and bind the rim.
- **Mats**: over-one-under-one — drying racks, bedding, door curtains.
- **Hide sewing**: bone needle + hemp thread; dense stitches (tight stitching is what
  keeps wind out and warmth in).

## 5. Pre-Departure Training Standards

- [ ] Bow drill: **80%+ success over 10 consecutive attempts**, in varied weather
- [ ] Knots: each of the four, **blindfolded, within 30 seconds**
- [ ] Plants: 30+ species identified in the field (botanical garden or wild); review the
      tables above for Central Plains destinations
- [ ] Pottery: one completed coil-built, bonfire-fired vessel
- [ ] Cordage: a cord that holds your body weight
